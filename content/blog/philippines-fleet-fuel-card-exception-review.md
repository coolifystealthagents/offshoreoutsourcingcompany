# Fleet Fuel-Card Exception Review for a Philippines Finance Support Team

*October 11, 2026*

Prepare explainable transaction exceptions while managers retain fraud, discipline, reimbursement, and payment decisions. The operating goal is a reviewable administrative lane: approved sources enter, routine checks happen under written rules, exceptions remain visible, and the person with authority makes the decision.

## Define an exception without declaring misconduct

Write observable rules such as purchase outside assigned region, quantity above vehicle capacity, prohibited product code, repeated transaction within a set interval, card used after assignment ended, missing odometer, or unmatched vehicle. Each rule needs a data source, threshold owner, effective date, exclusions, and routing path. A Philippines analyst can apply the rule and assemble facts. They cannot label fraud, accuse an employee, decide discipline, approve reimbursement, block a card, or reject payment unless separately authorized under a documented control.

## Freeze the transaction and assignment snapshots

Preserve the card record, vehicle assignment, driver assignment where permitted, merchant data, product code, quantity, amount, currency, timestamp, odometer input, receipt reference, and rule version. Rosters change, so a live lookup later may tell the wrong story. Normalize timezones and retain the source timestamp. Use stable identifiers rather than names in general reports. If a card is pooled or a vehicle reassigned mid-shift, mark the uncertainty instead of forcing the transaction onto the nearest person.

## Compare physical plausibility carefully

Tank capacity, fuel type, recent purchases, route, and telematics may help explain a transaction, but each source has limits. Auxiliary tanks, equipment fuel, replacements, rentals, delayed settlement, and merchant coding can make a normal purchase look unusual. The analyst should show the comparison and confidence, not reach a misconduct conclusion. Safety, HR, fleet, finance, and investigation owners decide next steps. Avoid contacting drivers with accusatory language; use an owner-approved neutral request when information is needed.

## Use a two-lane review queue

Separate data-quality defects from conduct or policy exceptions. Missing odometer, unknown vehicle ID, duplicated feed row, and merchant-code mismatch may require system or vendor correction. Out-of-policy time, location, product, or volume may require manager review. Give each lane different owners and service clocks. This prevents analysts from burying technical defects in employee queues and prevents managers from receiving raw false positives. Keep the original rule hit even when later evidence resolves it so threshold quality can be reviewed.

## Limit personal and location exposure

Fuel data can reveal employee routines and vehicle movement. Collect only what the approved review requires, restrict row-level access, and avoid publishing identifiable league tables. Set retention with privacy, HR, legal, and finance owners. GAO internal-control standards support documented responsibilities and quality information, but do not determine employee-monitoring legality or investigation procedure. The outsourced role should prepare evidence within the approved purpose and never browse transactions or routes out of curiosity.

## Investigate a capacity alert without accusation

A card transaction shows 42 gallons while the assigned van’s listed tank holds 31. The analyst checks the frozen vehicle roster, sees that the card was temporarily assigned to a truck, and finds a supervisor-approved assignment record. A second data issue remains: the fleet system was not updated until the next day. The analyst links the sources, resolves the policy alert under the written rule, and routes the delayed roster update to fleet data ownership. They do not write that the driver was cleared of fraud, because fraud was never theirs to determine.

## Score rule usefulness and resolution quality

Report alerts by rule, confirmed data defects, owner-determined policy outcomes, false positives, time to evidence packet, missing sources, and repeated root causes. Keep denominators for all transactions and applicable vehicles. Sample resolved alerts backward to source snapshots and owner decisions. Review whether one location, merchant, or shift creates systematic data issues. A strong process reduces unexplained exceptions and improves inputs; it does not maximize alert volume. Revalidate rules after fleet, card provider, fueling policy, telematics, or assignment-process changes.
